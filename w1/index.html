1 · Basics 2 min

Your capstone is a learning tool you will design across four milestones and defend at the showcase. Workshop 1 is the first of four, and it is about one thing: what the AI is, and what it is not.

Final project structureWhat it answers
M1 · nowwho the tool is for, and the problem it solves
M2how it teaches
M3how it motivates
M4who it leaves out
Showcasethe tool, and your defence of it

AI will help create the tool. You will decide who it is for, whether it is right, and whether it should exist. The production is outsourceable. The judgement is not.

If you are worried you cannot code: no one in this course writes code by hand. The code track means you read and run what the tool writes. The no-code track means you never see it. Both are legitimate, and both need Milestone 1 first.

3 · Activity: be the AI model 10 min

The video's key moment is at 0:36–0:51: the model assigns a probability to every possible next word, then samples one — it does not always take the most likely. For ten minutes, you are the model.

Train 4 min — five words that could fill the blank, and how likely each is in ordinary English. The five must add to 100.

Generate 3 min — the page draws a number from 1 to 100 and walks down your list until the probabilities add past it. That word is the output. Generate three times.

Write · 3 min
Read after your three rolls

Sentence C. Almost everyone's model says "note." The most probable word is not what happened — Maya passed a drawing. The model has never seen Maya draw; it has read a great many sentences about students passing things during exams. The tool produces the probable, not the true. Keep that line; it comes back in Milestone 1, Part 4.

The low-probability roll. When a 3% word comes up, the sentence still reads fine — "the cat sat on the keyboard." Fluent, plausible, and not what anyone expected. That is the video's point about natural-sounding text, and it is also how a hallucination reads: nothing in the output marks it as the unlikely pick.

Sentence D. Where did your probabilities come from? From what you have read and heard. So do the model's. If the internet mostly says "practice," the model says practice — whether or not that is true for a 9th grader who needs to see the shape before the formula.

Sentence E. What were your top words — transform, revolutionize, help, personalize? That is what has been written about AI and education, and it is what the tool will say back to you when you ask. Milestone 1 has to say something more specific than the probable word.

Prediction, not knowledge. A language model predicts the most likely next text, given what it has read. It has not met your learner. It has read a great many paragraphs about learners like the one you described, and it produces the most probable next paragraph.

Fluent ≠ true. Confident ≠ verifiable sources.

You have already used one. The Week 4 instrument is a stand-alone page: a model wrote the code, and your instructor checked every item against the published scale before it went to you. That is the division of labour in one object.

Code or no-code

No-code

  • a written role and rules (a system prompt)
  • a knowledge file you upload
  • a form that scores itself
  • an artifact you asked for and kept

You never see the code. The tool is the configuration.

Code

  • you ask for the HTML, JavaScript or Python
  • you run it, and it breaks, and you ask again
  • you own the file — it works without the chat

You never write the code. You read it, run it, and decide.

How this maps to Milestone 1, Part 3. Socratic tutor = chatbot tutor. Interactive simulation = artifact or stand-alone page. Practice/feedback generator = teaching assistant. Instrument = Forms + Sheets, or a static page. The track (code / no-code) is not this week's decision — M1 is the same either way.

The one distinction that matters now: who is the user — the learner, or you? A teaching-assistant tool is a legitimate capstone. It still needs a population, because the practice it generates is for someone.

5 · Prompts, tested 12 min

Vague vs. contextualized 4 min

Same tool, same question, twice. Copy each prompt into your chat tool and compare the two answers. AI output quality depends on the quality of the input — can you trust the answer?

Vague

Explain quadratic equations to a student.

Contextualized developmentally appropriate

A 9th grader can execute the quadratic formula but cannot say what the answer is. They learn from diagrams and hands-on work before text. In under 120 words, describe what the two solutions are before any formula.

Notice what the context is: what the learner can do, what she cannot yet, how she learns. Developmental facts, not "make it simple." You should recognise her — this is Maya. A contextualized prompt is a population statement, which is what Milestone 1 Parts 1 and 2 are.

Now pick one 8 min

Catch one

Ask for something real, at a level of detail the tool cannot actually have. (Made-up studies no longer work — today's models say they cannot find them. A real figure with a real source is where they still slip.)

What percentage of Mississippi 9th graders scored proficient on the Algebra I state test last year? Name the source.

Then open the source it names. Is that number there, for that year? If the tool hedges — "I can't verify this" — good: that is checkpoint 2 working on its side. It does not replace checkpoint 2 on yours.

Other prompts that still catch: a direct quote with a page number from a real textbook; the sample size and method of Gosling, Rentfrow & Swann (2003), the paper behind the Week 4 instrument.

Build one

Ask for a tiny tool for your learners, and make the tool tell you what it is unsure about.

Make a five-question quiz for [your learners — can ____, cannot yet ____]. Then tell me which question you are least sure fits them, and why.

Read the answer to the second sentence first. The tool will name a default assumption about your learners — reading level, prior knowledge, motivation. That is Milestone 1 Part 4, done for you.

Write

Hallucination, defined: fluent output with no source behind it — or with a source that does not say what the output says. The skill is not "AI lies." The skill is "I have a routine for checking," and it runs whether or not the tool sounds sure.

6 · Human agency 12 min

Everything so far is about what the tool does. This part is about what only you do. The framework is Tina Austin's UnBlooms™ — the reflective checkpoint process: five places to stop, with an exit at every one.

CheckpointThe question you stop and ask
1 · Intent & EntryWhat am I asking for, and why this tool?
2 · Generate & VerifyIs this true? Where and how would I check?
3 · Critique & JudgeDoes this fit my need — my learners, or the probable learner?
4 · Refine & DecideWhat do I change, and what do I keep?
5 · Create or ResistDo I build this — or does a simpler handout do the job?

At the centre: your disciplinary judgement. At every checkpoint: × Resist is an exit. The cycle is not a hierarchy and it is not a line — you can decline to prompt, decline to accept, decline to build. That is what agency in the centre means.

Reflection is the process. Pause at each checkpoint. Question the output. Make a deliberate choice. AI should support learning, not replace thinking.

You are responsible for the use and consequences of everything the AI gives you.

You have already been through the cycle this hour. Be the model was checkpoint 2 — what is this output, and is it the true one? Catch one was 2 and 3. Build one was 3 — the tool named its own default. Milestone 1 Part 3's "what a handout cannot do" is checkpoint 5.

Cognitive offloading

Handing a piece of thinking to something outside your head. It is not a sin — a calculator is offloading, and so is a textbook. The line is between production and judgement. AI is most useful when it augments what you can do for your students.

Offload the productionthe HTML, the fifty practice items, the three reading levels, the first draft of the rubric
Keep the judgementwho the learner is · whether the output is true · whether it fits · whether to build at all
Be present for the learnerif you have gained time, that is time for personal interaction and relationship building

You cannot use a tool well if you do not know what you asked it to do — or if you accept whatever it produces. Milestone 1 exists so the judgement is written down before any production starts: the population is yours, in your words, and the tool inherits it.

How to prompt, using the checkpoints

CheckpointHabits to decide whether AI is appropriate or not
IntentWrite the ask in one line before opening the tab: who · can/cannot yet · what output. If you cannot determine this, you are not ready to prompt.
GenerateGive it the learner, not the age. Ask for under N words. Ask it to name what it is least sure about.
VerifyHow do you know it is true? One claim, checked by you, every time. Sources named; sources opened.
Critique"Which of your assumptions about these learners could be wrong?" — then decide if it is right.
RefineRe-prompt with what it got wrong, not with "try again."
DecideKeep, change, or resist. Say which, and why, in your own words.

"In your own words" is not a style preference. From Milestone 2 on, your documentation log asks for the decision and the reason at each checkpoint — and a reason pasted from the tool is a reason you did not make.

Two versions of you

Competent and ethical userNot ready for AI
Used AI as a toolOutsourced the thinking
Struggled, failed, revised, improvedNever sat with the confusion
Knows what good reasoning feels likeGuiding others through confusion they never had

The tool can do your homework. It cannot do your rehearsal — and if it did the homework, you likely did not learn at all. The capstone is student teaching before student teaching — feed the tool a unit you will teach, make it roleplay your ten hardest learners, make it write fifty exit tickets for you to answer.

Then: Milestone 1, at home

One page, five parts. The instructions and the rubric are in the Week 5 module in Canvas; the milestone itself is done in Packback. Open the instructions now, while the hour is fresh.

  1. Who — a class, a group, or one person; observed behaviours, not labels
  2. The problem — can ____ but cannot yet ____, because ____
  3. The tool type — and what it does that a handout cannot
  4. Assumptions blind spot — one default the AI would have about your learners, and how you can tell
  5. Synthesis — what Unit 1 predicts they will do with it

"Learner" is whoever you will actually work with. Teachers take a class and name the two or three learners who stretch it. Counsellors, coaches and social workers take a group. Nurses, therapists and tutors take one learner, patient or client. Your exit card is Parts 1, 2 and 4 in three lines — start from it.

Peer feedback is part of the milestone grade: once drafts are posted, you critically challenge a partner's draft and provide suggestions in the four boxes, and add one line at the end of your own on what you changed. The peer-feedback page in the module has the four boxes; check the module for the deadline.
