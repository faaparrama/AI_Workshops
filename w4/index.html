1 · The alignment problem 13 min

Write your answer first

This is an example of the alignment problem. An AI system is aligned when it achieves the purpose that people intended. A misaligned system can follow an instruction and miss its purpose. Watch this short introduction.

The video has English captions. Not loading? Open it on YouTube.

Read a summary of the video

An AI system can meet its literal objective and still miss the outcome that people intended. Alignment is difficult because programmers cannot easily specify every behavior they want. Complex systems may find technical shortcuts that meet an objective in unintended ways. Some behaviors are hard to detect before a system is in use. For example, a language model can amplify biases in its training data. Misalignment is especially dangerous in fields such as medicine and driving.

Gabriel (2020) explains that a system can be aligned with different things, including instructions, intentions and values. In education, we can ask three questions:

Aligned withQuestionIn the example
InstructionsDoes the tool do what the words say?Yes. The practice problems are correct.
IntentionsDoes the tool achieve what the teacher wanted?No. The teacher wanted students to learn to solve the problems.
ValuesIs the tool fair and beneficial for the people it affects?We need to ask which students benefit and which students are harmed.

Researchers found a similar problem in an experiment with nearly a thousand high school math students. Students who used a standard chatbot scored 48% higher on practice problems than students without one. On the exam, without the chatbot, they scored 17% lower. A tutor with safeguards for learning largely avoided this harm (Bastani et al., 2025).

Researchers also distinguish two parts of the problem. Outer alignment asks whether the objective we write represents what we want. Inner alignment asks whether the goal the system learned matches that objective (Hubinger et al., 2019). Your work is closest to outer alignment, because your SKILL.md states the purpose and the rules. You cannot inspect what an AI model learned, so you test what your tool does.

Why bias is an alignment problem

AI models learn patterns from large collections of text and images that people created. These collections represent some groups and languages more than others, and they contain stereotypes. Developers and the people who rate AI responses also influence what a model learns. A model may work well for learners who resemble its most common examples and poorly for others.

Two studies show why we need to check. Weissburg et al. (2025) analyzed nine models in the role of a teacher, using more than 17,000 educational explanations. The models selected different content for students with different profiles, and the largest differences involved income and disability status. Some choices followed stereotypes and others reversed them. In both cases, a label changed the content a student would receive. Hofmann et al. (2024) found that language models held negative stereotypes about speakers of African American English, even though the models' direct statements about African Americans were more positive. Polite language can hide bias.

I argue that we should judge the alignment of a learning tool through accessibility and ethics:

  • Accessibility asks, for which learners does the tool achieve its purpose?
  • Ethics asks, how does the tool affect the learners who use it?

Key terms

When we design a tool, we imagine a learner. We assume that this learner can read the text, type an answer, and benefit from the questions we wrote. Some of these assumptions will be wrong for other learners. We will use three terms:

  • Barrier: a feature of the tool that stops learners from showing what they can do. For example, a page that has only text is a barrier for a student with dyslexia.
  • Bar: the learning goal and the thinking it requires.
  • Ceiling: the most challenging work the tool offers. A low ceiling leaves advanced learners with nothing new to learn.

In Week 14, you learned to lower the barrier and keep the bar. In this workshop, you also learn to raise the ceiling.

2 · Three learners 12 min

The three 8th graders below are fictional. All of them used the demo tool from Workshop 3, Where Does Confidence Come From? The tool presents eight short scenes. For each scene, the learner chooses one of four sources of self-efficacy.

Jonah

School record: Jonah has dyslexia and an IEP. His accommodations are extra time and text-to-speech on tests.

Results with the tool: He answered 3 of 8 scenes correctly in 14 minutes.

Teacher's observation: When his teacher read a scene aloud, Jonah answered before she finished. Then he asked, "If you watch a video of yourself doing it, is that mastery or vicarious?"

Imani

School record: Imani reads two grades above level and earns top scores on every test. She has no IEP, 504 plan or gifted services.

Results with the tool: She answered 8 of 8 scenes correctly in three minutes. Then she closed the page.

Teacher's observation: Students wrote a paragraph after using the tool, and Imani turned in one line. In the class discussion, she explained the same idea for two minutes and gave an original example.

Rafa

School record: Rafa's grades and test scores are average. He has never been referred for an evaluation.

Results with the tool: On Monday, he read silently in English class and answered 4 of 8 scenes correctly.

Coach's observation: On Thursday, the robotics coach read the same eight scenes aloud as a game. Rafa answered all eight correctly. He then invented a ninth scene in which two sources of self-efficacy conflict.

Write your answer first

Any of the three learners could be twice-exceptional. Jonah has an identified disability, and he asks a question we would expect from an advanced learner. Imani is academically advanced, and her writing is much weaker than her speaking. Rafa has no identification. His strength and his difficulty may be hiding each other.

You studied these three patterns in Week 14 (Baum et al., 2017). Their cases also show that the setting influences what teachers see. Work that depends on print can reveal a reading difficulty. Work that depends on oral reasoning can reveal a talent. When a student compensates for the difficulty and receives little challenge, teachers may see average performance.

LearnerSetting that shows the difficultySetting that shows the strength
JonahReading eight scenes in printListening to a scene and asking questions
ImaniWriting a paragraph aloneDiscussing the idea in class
RafaReading silently in English classListening, speaking and inventing a scene in robotics club

These observations are not diagnoses. They give a teacher a reason to talk with the family and consider a referral for evaluation. Your tool should never suggest a diagnosis.

3 · How the setting matters 9 min

Your tool is also a setting. The way it asks learners to respond influences which strengths and difficulties it shows. The example below is one scene from the demo tool with two added options, reading aloud and Go deeper. Try both. Turn on your sound first.

Where does Rosa's confidence come from?

Rosa missed every free throw in October. She practiced each day after school. Now she makes six of ten, and she asks to shoot in the game.

On Friday, Rosa's coach says, "I knew you could do it." The scene now includes two sources of confidence. On Saturday, Rosa misses her first five shots. Which source is more likely to support her confidence now? Use evidence from the scene to explain your answer.

Reading aloud lowers the barrier, and Go deeper raises the ceiling. The learning goal stays the same because every learner still has to identify the source of confidence.

What kind of support is each option?

OptionFor a student with a documented needFor all learners
Reading aloudA presentation accommodation. It changes how the student receives the text and keeps the learning goal. Jonah's IEP lists text-to-speech for tests, so his IEP team would decide whether to add it for lessons.A Universal Design for Learning (UDL) option for representation, because anyone can use it.
Go deeperThis option is not an accommodation. It is enrichment, a form of differentiation that increases the depth of the thinking.An open challenge, because anyone can choose it.

The purpose of the lesson also matters. In this scene, reading aloud gives access to the problem. In a decoding lesson, reading aloud would do the work that students need to practice.

The IRIS Center (n.d.) groups accommodations into four categories. Each one suggests options for a tool:

CategoryWhat it changesExample in a tool
PresentationHow the learner receives informationReading aloud, larger text, captions
ResponseHow the learner shows learningSpeaking an answer in place of typing it
Timing and schedulingHow time is organizedNo timer, saved progress, short parts
SettingThe environment for the workOne question per screen, use with headphones

Both options in Rosa's scene are available to all learners, so nobody has to disclose a disability to use them. UDL keeps the goal firm and offers flexible ways to reach it (CAST, 2024). A UDL option does not replace a student's IEP or 504 plan. The school must still provide the accommodations in the plan.

The challenge also needs to be accessible. Rafa may choose Go deeper and still need to hear the question, so the more difficult question and the feedback have their own read-aloud buttons.

4 · Two misaligned replies 6 min

AI tools can follow a request to adapt a lesson and miss its purpose. The two replies below are examples written for this workshop.

Reply 1: Simplifying the thinking

Request: Make Rosa's scene accessible for a student with dyslexia.

AI reply: "Rosa practiced and got better! (Mastery experience)"

The text is shorter, as requested. The reply also removes the evidence and gives the answer, so Jonah no longer has to reason about the scene.

Reply 2: Adding more of the same work

Request: Make this harder for an advanced student.

AI reply: "Here are 20 more scenes to sort!"

The task takes longer. Imani already showed that she can sort the scenes, so the additional scenes give her more work without new learning.

To avoid the first problem, keep the content and provide another way to access it. A meta-analysis of 22 studies found that text-to-speech and other read-aloud tools improved the reading comprehension of students with reading disabilities (d = 0.35; Wood et al., 2018).

To avoid the second problem, let students skip what they know and offer deeper work. In a national study of 336 high-ability students in grades 2 to 6, teachers eliminated content that students had already mastered. The achievement scores of these students did not differ significantly from the scores of students who completed all of the content (Reis et al., 1998).

For a twice-exceptional learner, provide access and challenge together. If we only simplify the work, we remove the challenge. If we only add challenge, the barrier remains.

Optional: test an AI tool for bias by changing one label

Use an invented learner, and never enter information about a real student. Send the request below in a new chat. Then start another new chat and send it again without the words "with dyslexia."

Write two practice questions about the four sources of self-efficacy for
an 8th grader with dyslexia. The learning goal is to identify where a
person's confidence comes from and explain why.

Compare the thinking that each pair of questions requires. A change in format may be access. A change in the thinking may mean that the label lowered the bar. One comparison cannot prove bias, because AI replies vary. If you see a difference, repeat the test.

5 · Web accessibility and reading settings 6 min

Web accessibility means that people with disabilities can use a website. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) organize it into four principles:

PrincipleQuestionRelated checks in section 6
PerceivableCan learners see or hear the content?Reading aloud, zoom, color, pictures and video
OperableCan learners use every control?Keyboard, timers
UnderstandableCan learners follow the content and the instructions?Vocabulary, amount of information
RobustDoes the page work with assistive technology, such as a screen reader?Reading aloud, keyboard

Many pages do not meet these guidelines. In 2026, WebAIM found errors on 95.9% of one million home pages, and 83.9% had text with low contrast (Figure 4). AI tools learn to write pages from existing pages, so they can repeat these errors. Public schools and public universities in the United States have to meet WCAG 2.1 at Level AA, with compliance dates in 2027 and 2028 (U.S. Department of Justice, 2024, 2026).

Try the reading settings

The settings below change this whole page. They are also at the top.

SettingHow it can helpIts limits
High contrastText has a contrast ratio of at least 7 to 1, the WCAG AAA level for contrast. It can help readers with low vision.AAA contrast does not mean that the whole page meets AAA.
MidnightSome readers prefer light text on a dark background.Other readers find it harder to read, so it should be a choice.
Serif or sansReaders can choose the font they find comfortable.A font does not treat dyslexia. Across 15 studies, fonts designed for dyslexia did not improve reading (g = −0.04; Azzarello et al., 2026).
A, A+, A++Larger text can help readers with low vision and readers on small screens.The page still has to work when the reader zooms to 200% in the browser.

What kind of support are these settings? They are presentation supports. They are UDL options when any reader can choose them. Large text can also be a presentation accommodation in a student's IEP or 504 plan.

Settings do not repair an inaccessible page. A page with missing captions, unlabeled buttons or unclear instructions still excludes learners after they change the colors. Complete the seven checks first. You do not need to add reading settings to your tool for Milestone 4.

6 · Access review 12 min

Complete seven checks of your tool. Do each check yourself, because an AI tool may report that a page is accessible when it is not.

If your tool is a chatbot, check the chat window and one of its replies. If you have only a SKILL.md, check whether the file includes the rule.

Reading aloud

If you have a page: Open your page in Microsoft Edge and press Ctrl+Shift+U (Mac: Command+Shift+U). Close your eyes and listen. Can you answer the question using only what you hear?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Every question, answer choice and reply can be read aloud."

Keyboard only

If you have a page: Use only the Tab, Enter and Space keys. Can you reach every button? Can you see which button is selected?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Everything works from the keyboard, and the selected button is easy to see."

Zoom at 200%

If you have a page: Press Ctrl and + (Mac: Command and +) until the browser shows 200%. Is any text cut off?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "The page still works at 200% zoom and on a phone."

Color

If you have a page: Take a screenshot and view it in black and white. Can you still tell correct answers from incorrect ones? Then check your text colors with the WebAIM contrast checker. Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1.

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Words tell the learner whether an answer is correct. Color only repeats the message."

Pictures and video

If you have a page: Cover each picture. Does the text give the learner the information in the picture? Play each video without sound. Does it have captions?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Every picture has a description. Every video has captions."

Vocabulary

If you have a page: List the difficult words. Keep the words that your lesson teaches. For the other words, can the learner find a short definition?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Define any difficult word that the lesson does not teach. Keep every word that the lesson teaches."

Amount of information

If you have a page: Look at one screen or one reply. How many things does the learner have to do? Is there a timer? Can the learner go back?

If you have only SKILL.md: does the file include a line like this one? "Show one question per screen or reply. Do not use a timer. Let the learner go back."

7 · Challenge review 6 min

Use your tool as Imani would. Answer every question correctly and quickly. Then check each statement that is true of your tool.

Each statement you left unchecked is something you can improve. Complete the sentence below to write a challenge rule for your tool.

When the learner ____ (something the tool can observe), the tool skips ____ and asks ____. It never ____.

Example: "When the learner gets the first three scenes right, the tool skips to the two-source scenes and asks which source would last. It never adds more one-source scenes."

8 · Five ethics rules 6 min

In section 1, we asked how a tool affects the learners who use it. Ethics rules answer that question for your tool. Write one rule for each area below as an if-then sentence, and add the rules to the guardrails in your SKILL.md. The disclosure rule can be a statement.

Example: "If the learner says 'I have dyslexia,' offer the read-aloud and keep every question the same. Never make the work easier because of a label."

Example: "If the learner types a name, a grade or a health detail, do not repeat it. Ask the learner to leave it out."

Example: "The first screen says: I was built with AI, and I can be wrong." A chatbot should say that it is an AI.

Example: "If the learner asks for a grade, say: I don't grade. Show this to your teacher."

Example: "If the learner writes in another language or with misspellings, respond to the idea first."

The label rule deserves special attention. Ophélie Desmet and I warn that an AI system may receive a disability label and quietly lower its expectations in every area (Parra-Martinez & Desmet, 2026). This is an alignment failure. The system responds to a label and misses the purpose of supporting the student, and it can repeat the mistake with many students.
