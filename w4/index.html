1 · The deal 3 min

Every set of rules has a learner in mind. Look at your LEARNER block. Someone reads fast enough, types well enough, and knows just little enough to need your tool. That is your default learner.

Other learners will open it too. Some meet a barrier: they can do the thinking, and the screen stops them. Some hit a ceiling: they already know it, and the tool has nothing left to ask. Some meet both on the same screen.

Last week's rule still holds: lower the barrier, keep the bar. Today adds a second half: raise the ceiling.

2 · Three learners 12 min

I invented these three 8th graders. All of them use the demo tool from Workshop 3, Where Does Confidence Come From?: eight short scenes, four buttons.

Jonah

The file says: dyslexia, with an IEP. Extra time, and text-to-speech on tests.

The tool saw: 3 of 8. Fourteen minutes.

His teacher saw: she read a scene aloud to him. He answered before she finished. Then he asked, "If you watch a video of yourself doing it, is that mastery or vicarious?"

Imani

The file says: reads two grades ahead. Top scores on every test. No plan of any kind.

The tool saw: 8 of 8. Three minutes. She closed the tab.

Her teacher saw: a paragraph was due after the tool. Imani turned in one line. In class she had talked about the same idea for two minutes, with an example nobody else had.

Rafa

The file says: average. Never referred for anything.

The tool saw, Monday, in English class, reading silently: 4 of 8.

The coach saw, Thursday, in robotics club: she read the same eight scenes aloud as a game. Rafa got all eight. He made up a ninth, with two sources pulling in opposite directions.

Commit first

All three files leave the question open. Jonah's file names a disability, and his question is one an advanced student asks. Imani's file names an advanced student, and the writing doesn't match the talking. Rafa's file names nothing: each side may be hiding the other.

You met the three patterns in Week 14 (Baum et al., 2017). The new piece is the setting. A setting that asks for print shows the reading. A setting that asks for reasoning out loud shows the talent. A setting that asks for neither shows "average."

3 · Your tool is a setting 7 min

What your tool asks for decides which learner it sees. Here is one scene from the demo, as it was, with two options added. Try all of it.

Where does Rosa's confidence come from?

Rosa missed every free throw in October. She practiced each day after school. Now she makes six of ten, and she asks to shoot in the game.

On Friday her coach says, "I knew you could." Now two sources are in the scene. Rosa misses her first five shots on Saturday. Which source is still holding her up, and what in the scene tells you?

Read aloud lowers the barrier. Go deeper raises the ceiling. The four sources never changed: that is the bar.

Look at who has to ask. Nobody. Both buttons are open to everyone, and the tool never learns who has an IEP. That is Universal Design for Learning: firm goals, flexible means (CAST, 2024).

Now the test for Rafa. He opens Go deeper. The harder question can be read aloud too. If access stops where the challenge starts, the tool serves one half of him at a time.

4 · Two traps 5 min

Ask an AI to adapt something and it often falls into one of these. I wrote both replies as examples of what to watch for.

Trap 1 · It only shortens

You: Make Rosa's scene accessible for a student with dyslexia.

Reply: "Rosa practiced and got better! (Mastery experience)"

The evidence is gone. The answer is in the text. Jonah has nothing left to think about.

Trap 2 · It only adds

You: Make this harder for an advanced student.

Reply: "Here are 20 more scenes to sort!"

Imani got eight right in three minutes. Twenty more is a penalty for knowing it.

For Trap 1, keep the text and add a route. Across 22 studies, text-to-speech and other read-aloud tools helped students with reading disabilities understand what they read, d = 0.35 (Wood et al., 2018).

For Trap 2, skip what they know and go deeper. In a national study of 336 high-ability students, grades 2 to 6, teachers skipped the work that students already knew. Their achievement scores did not differ significantly from those of students who did all of it (Reis et al., 1998).

For a 2e learner, do both at once. A rule that only simplifies takes away the challenge. A rule that only adds challenge leaves the barrier in place.

5 · Audit 1: the barrier 12 min

Seven checks. Do each one with your own eyes, ears and hands. "The AI said it's accessible" doesn't count.

Your tool is a chatbot? Run the checks on the chat window and on one of its replies. You have only SKILL.md? That's fine: look for the rule.

Listen to it

You have a page: Open your page in Microsoft Edge and press Ctrl+Shift+U (Mac: Command+Shift+U). Close your eyes. Could you answer from sound alone?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "Every question, answer choice and reply can be read aloud."

Put the mouse away

You have a page: Use Tab, Enter and Space only. Can you reach every button, and can you see which one you are on?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "Everything works from the keyboard, and the selected button is easy to see."

Zoom to 200%

You have a page: Press Ctrl and + (Mac: Command and +) until the browser says 200%. Does any text get cut off?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "The page still works at 200% zoom and on a phone."

Take the color away

You have a page: Take a screenshot and view it in black and white. Can you still tell right from wrong? Check your text colors with the WebAIM contrast checker: normal text needs 4.5 to 1.

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "Right and wrong are said in words. Color only repeats it."

Pictures and video

You have a page: Cover each picture with your hand. Does the text say what the learner needs from it? Play each video with no sound. Are there captions?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "Every picture has a description. Every video has captions."

Hard words

You have a page: Circle the hard words. Keep the ones your lesson teaches. For each of the others, can the learner get a short meaning?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "Define any hard word the lesson does not teach. Never replace a word the lesson does teach."

One thing at a time

You have a page: Count what is on one screen, or in one reply. Is there a timer? Can the learner go back?

You have only SKILL.md: is this line in it? "One question per screen or reply. No timer. The learner can go back."

6 · Audit 2: the ceiling 6 min

Play Imani. Get everything right, fast.

Anything unchecked is a line to write. Fill the blanks.

When the learner ____ (something the tool can see), the tool skips ____ and asks ____. It never ____.

Example: "When the learner gets the first three scenes right, the tool skips to the two-source scenes and asks which source would last. It never adds more one-source scenes."

7 · Five ethics rules 6 min

Access asks who can get in. Ethics asks what the tool does to the people who do. Write each rule as an if-then line, under your guardrails.

Example: "If the learner says 'I have dyslexia,' offer the read-aloud and keep every question the same. Never make the work easier because of a label."

Example: "If the learner types a name, a grade or a health detail, do not repeat it. Ask them to leave it out."

Example: "The first screen says: I was built with AI, and I can be wrong." A chatbot says it is an AI.

Example: "If the learner asks for a grade, say: I don't grade. Show this to your teacher."

Example: "If the learner writes in another language or with misspellings, respond to the idea first."

The label rule is the one I care about most. Desmet and I warn about a system that sees a disability label and quietly lowers expectations in everything (Parra-Martinez & Desmet, 2026). A tool can do that to a whole school before anyone notices.
